About Outcomes Problem Research Learnings

Vertice

Period

2025 – Present

Role

Senior Product Designer

Team

Across 3 engineering teams & 3 product managers

Responsibility

End to end: research to development

About

Vertice helps companies buy and renew their software at the best price, saving them millions. The platform is where teams track what they already own and ask for what they need next. Since it was built it had been rigid, and organised around its own model rather than around the people using it. That is where I came in. I own the experience end to end, from someone’s first time in the product through to an approval that satisfies compliance.

Outcomes

The work runs across the platform rather than sitting in one corner of it. I designed first time onboarding, and a global search that reaches vendors, contracts and navigation from a single field, so people stop hunting through a menu for something they could simply ask for.

I rebuilt the request page around what people are trying to achieve rather than around the objects sitting behind it, and took it from there towards a flexible requisition model: someone starts from a business, category or employee need and takes whichever route fits, instead of being marched down a single path. The negotiation process brings AI into the work itself, and approvals now bend to the shape of the company running them rather than putting every company through the same chain.

Problem

The platform was inflexible, and it described its own data model rather than the way procurement actually works. Tracking anything was harder than it should have been, people could not get their job done without working around the product, and what they saw on screen never matched the process they were running in real life. Adoption struggled, which is the plainest signal a platform can give you that it has been built from the inside out.

Research

Three sources, each answering something the others could not. PostHog told me what people actually did, and that is where the case for expanding search came from: the quickest way to cut time to value was to stop making people navigate for things they could ask for directly.

Interviews with procurement managers, inside the company and outside it, told me why they behaved that way, and gave me enough confidence to make quick decisions and stand behind them. AI prototyping closed the last gap, putting something working in front of people early on features like the negotiation agent, so we were responding to how it felt to use rather than to how it read on a slide.

Learnings

Flexibility beats rigidity, and it is worth the extra work. A rigid flow is easy to specify, easy to build and easy to defend to compliance, but people do not work in straight lines and they need room to change their minds. The flexible version improved the experience ten times over and, against expectation, made the whole process easier to track rather than harder.

Designing with AI asks for the problem to be stated more clearly than designing without it. A prototype comes back in minutes, but only ever as good as the definition it was given, so the thinking has to happen first: what the user problem actually is, and which details genuinely matter.

In procurement, use cases and permissions are the work rather than the edge cases. The data is sensitive enough that getting a permission wrong is not a rough edge to tidy up later, it is the whole problem.

And stay close to engineering. When there is more product being built than there is design to cover it, the most useful thing a designer can do is make sure the people building it have direction, rather than waiting to review what comes out.
